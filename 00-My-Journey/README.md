# My Homelab Journey

## Why I Built It

I started this homelab while studying cybersecurity in college. The shared school labs were useful, but they were not mine. If I broke a server while experimenting, I had to wait for someone else to repair or restore it before I could continue.

I learn by trying things repeatedly until I understand them. I needed an environment where failure was allowed, recovery was my responsibility, and no one else's work or data was at risk.

A YouTube video about building a Proxmox homelab made me realize that an unused gaming PC could become that environment. I worked within a limited budget, bought a Cat6 cable and a small managed switch, added storage, and began building.

## How It Evolved

At first, the server was only meant for schoolwork and practice. I did not expect the infrastructure itself to become the part I enjoyed most.

I spent hours configuring virtual machines, troubleshooting mistakes, rebuilding systems, and learning how services interacted. The good days and the frustrating ones were both valuable because I had ownership of the entire problem.

The turning point came when my fiancée wanted to play Minecraft and asked whether we should rent another hosted server. I decided to build one myself instead. Once that first game server worked, the homelab stopped being only a classroom replacement and became a platform that solved real problems.

Now, whenever friends or family want a private game server, I can deploy and manage it myself. Each deployment becomes faster, more reliable, and better documented than the one before it.

## What It Is Today

Today, the homelab is my personal infrastructure platform.

It gives me the freedom to:

- host private game servers without paying a hosting company,
- build Linux and Windows lab environments,
- practice networking and cybersecurity safely,
- test automation and monitoring,
- break and restore systems without risking other people's data,
- and document real projects for my career portfolio.

The most valuable part is not the hardware. It is the freedom to learn by doing.

## What It Taught Me

This project taught me what I am capable of.

At the beginning, I was not confident that I could build or manage infrastructure like this. Every successful deployment, failed backup, broken configuration, recovery, and troubleshooting session reinforced the same lesson:

> If I am willing to keep learning and refuse to give up, I can solve the problem.

I do not know everything, and I never will. What I have proven is that I can approach difficult technology methodically, stay with the problem, and continue improving until I understand it.

I no longer look at a difficult problem and ask whether I can solve it. I ask: **What is the first step?**

## Looking Ahead

My long-term goal is to grow this environment until it resembles the infrastructure of a small business: reliable virtualization, networking, storage, backups, monitoring, automation, security, and documentation working together as one system.

I want employers to see more than a collection of servers. I want this repository to demonstrate curiosity, persistence, planning, troubleshooting, and the ability to design, operate, and improve real infrastructure.

This homelab will never truly be finished. Every new technology, mistake, and completed project becomes another opportunity to improve it.

## Why I Recommend Homelabbing

Homelabbing gives people freedom.

A project that begins with "I want to host a Minecraft server" quickly leads to questions about Linux, networking, backups, monitoring, security, automation, storage, and documentation.

That is why I jokingly describe homelabbing as a gateway into computer science: one project leads to another, and every new problem exposes another part of how computers and infrastructure work.

Eventually, you stop following tutorials and start designing your own solutions.
